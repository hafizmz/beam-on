Mentari’s Mission

How the hospital crew finds and treats cancer

Written by Hafiz Zin
Draft edition, November 2026

Mentari the sun bear loves three things: climbing, honey, and Big Bear.

One morning her tummy feels funny. There is a lump that was not there before.

Mentari feels puzzled.

“Big Bear,” she says, “come and feel this.”

What do you do when something feels funny in your body?
Curious corner
Our bodies change as we grow. If you find something new, like a lump, telling a grown-up is exactly the right thing to do.
2
3

At the hospital, Mentari feels worried. She holds Big Bear’s paw tight.

A doctor kneels down to say hello.

“I’m Dr. Sina. I’m a cancer doctor, and this is my crew. We will find it. We will check it. We will treat it. Together.”

How many helpers can you count in the crew?
Curious corner
A cancer doctor is called an oncologist. More than ten different jobs work together to care for one patient.
4
5

“Your body is made of tiny building blocks called cells,” says Dr. Sina. “Cells make copies, and then they stop. One of your cells forgot to stop.”

Mentari whispers, “Did I do something wrong?”

“No,” says Dr. Sina. “It is nobody’s fault. And nobody can catch it from you.”

Can you spot the cells that keep on copying?
Curious corner
Your body makes millions of new cells every day to help you grow and heal. Cancer starts when one cell stops listening to the “stop” signal and makes too many copies. Together they can form a lump.
6
7

First, the crew must find the lump.

Mentari lies on a bed that slides into a big ring. “Still as a sleeping bear,” says the radiographer.

Round and round goes the X-ray camera. Whirr!

On the screen, the computer draws a picture of the inside of Mentari’s tummy.

Find it.

Can you lie as still as Mentari while you count to five?
Curious corner
This is a CT scanner. X-rays pass through the body from many directions, and a computer puts the measurements together into a slice picture. Bone looks white and air looks black.
8
9

Next comes a camera that uses sound.

“This gel is cold and squishy,” says the radiographer. Mentari giggles.

A little wand slides over her tummy. It sends sound in and listens for the echo.

“There,” says the radiographer. “That round shape is the lump.”

Have you ever heard your own echo?
Curious corner
Ultrasound uses sound too high for us to hear. The sound bounces back from the parts inside, and the echoes make a picture. Sound cannot cross air, so the gel fills the gap.
10
11

Two more cameras to go.

The magnet camera goes knock, knock, knock! Mentari wears headphones.

For the glow camera, the nurse says, “One small, quick pinch.” Mentari squeezes Big Bear’s paw and feels brave.

Special sugar goes into her blood, and the busy cells light up.

What helps you feel brave?
Curious corner
An MRI scanner uses a strong magnet and radio waves, and no X-rays. A PET scanner follows a special sugar. Busy cells, like cancer cells, use more sugar, so they glow. The brain glows too, because it is always busy.
12
13

Now the crew must check the lump.

Mentari has sleepy medicine, so she feels nothing. The doctor takes a piece of the lump, as tiny as a grain of rice.

In the lab, the pathologist adds purple dye, then pink dye. Now the cells are easy to see.

Check it.

Which colour went on first?
Curious corner
Taking a tiny sample is called a biopsy. The purple dye sticks to the middle of each cell, called the nucleus. The pink dye colours the rest.
14
15

Mentari peeks into the microscope.

“These cells are neat, like tiles on a floor,” says the pathologist. “And these ones are messy, with big, dark middles. Those are the confused cells.”

Now the crew knows exactly what the lump is.

Point to the neat cells. Now point to the messy ones.
Curious corner
A pathologist is a doctor who studies cells. Cancer cells often have a nucleus that is large, dark and oddly shaped. Knowing the kind of cell helps the crew choose the right treatment.
16
17

The whole crew sits around one big table. Everyone brings a tool: a scan picture, a microscope slide, a beam plan, a medicine chart.

Mentari asks, “What happens next?”

“Three treatments will help you,” says Dr. Sina. “Surgery, radiotherapy and medicine. And we will do them together.”

Whose tool is whose? Match each helper to a tool.
Curious corner
The crew meets to choose the best plan for each patient. Some patients need one treatment, and some need two or three. Treatments often work better together.
18
19

Surgery day. The anaesthetist gives Mentari sleepy medicine through a soft mask. She sleeps and feels nothing.

The surgeon gently takes out the lump, with a little healthy edge all around, then stitches the spot closed.

When Mentari wakes up, her tummy is a bit sore, and Big Bear is right there.

Treat it.

Who was there when Mentari woke up?
Curious corner
Taking a little healthy edge helps make sure no confused cells are left behind. A doctor called Ibnu Sina wrote about this idea 1,000 years ago, and surgeons still follow it today.
20
21

Next is radiotherapy. Mentari must be a statue again.

The big machine turns around her and sends invisible beams from different sides. They all meet at one spot. She cannot feel them at all.

“All done for today!” Mentari jumps down, proud as can be, and Big Bear gives her a big hug. She will come back for a little more each day.

Can you be a statue until I say “all done”?
Curious corner
Each beam is gentle on its own. Where the beams cross, they add up and stop the confused cells. The medical physicist plans the beams and checks the machine. The beams are only there while the machine is on, so hugs are safe.
22
23

“Where does my medicine come from?” asks Mentari. Dr. Sina shows her a lab.

A scientist puts the same number of drops into every dish. One medicine is too weak. One hurts the healthy cells too. One calms the confused cells and is gentle on the healthy ones.

“That one,” says the scientist, “is worth more tests.”

Which dish would you choose? Why?
Curious corner
In a fair test, you change only one thing and keep everything else the same. New medicines are tested many times, over many years, before any patient is given them.
24
25

Medicine day. The nurse reads Mentari’s name band. “Right bear, right medicine!” Drip, drip, drip.

The medicine travels in her blood to find confused cells, wherever they are. Mentari feels tired, and her fur falls out for a while.

She picks a cosy yellow beanie. Then come rest weeks, with naps, stories and Big Bear.

Which beanie would you choose for Mentari?
Curious corner
This medicine is called chemotherapy. It works on cells that copy quickly. Hair cells copy quickly too, so hair may fall out for a while and then grow back. Rest weeks give healthy cells time to recover.
26
27

After many visits, Mentari feels much better. Her fur is growing back, soft and new.

She will come back for check-ups, and the crew will be here.

Mentari climbs her favourite tree. Big Bear waves from below.

Find it. Check it. Treat it. Together.

Can you find the honeybee one last time? Who would you like to be in the crew?
Curious corner
Many children with cancer get better, and treatments keep improving because scientists keep asking questions. Maybe one day you will be one of them.

For grown-ups

Talking about cancer with young children

  • 1Use the real word. Say “cancer”, and explain it simply: some cells got confused and kept making copies.
  • 2Keep answers short and honest. It is fine to say “I don’t know. Let’s ask the doctor.”
  • 3Say these two things often. Cancer is not catching, and it is nobody’s fault.
  • 4Be truthful about what will be felt. Scans and radiotherapy cannot be felt. A needle is a small, quick pinch.
  • 5Let your child lead. Answer the question asked, then pause. Feelings often come out in play and drawing.
  • 6Keep routines and stay close. Being apart from you is often the biggest worry.
About getting better. Many children with cancer get better. How things go depends on the type of cancer and the care available, so ask your child’s care team about your own situation. This book explains; it does not give medical advice.

Who was Ibnu Sina?

Ibnu Sina was a doctor who lived about 1,000 years ago, from 980 to 1037. He wrote a huge book called The Canon of Medicine. Doctors learned from it for hundreds of years.
He wrote that a growing lump should be taken out early and completely. He also wrote rules for testing medicines. Our Dr. Sina is named after him.

Picture words

Cell
A tiny building block of your body.
Lump
Many cells bunched together.
Cancer
Cells that forgot how to stop copying.
Scan
A picture of the inside of you.
X-ray
Light we cannot see that passes through the body.
Echo
Sound that bounces back.
Magnet
It pulls and lines things up.
Microscope
It makes tiny things look big.
Dye
Colour that helps us see cells.
Surgery
Taking the lump out while you sleep.
Radiotherapy
Beams that meet at one spot.
Chemotherapy
Medicine that travels in the blood.

Try it at home

Echo game

Clap in a big room, then in a small room. Which one echoes? Sound pictures work the same way.

Statue game

Who can stay as still as a statue for 10 seconds? Staying still helps scans and radiotherapy work well.

Fair test

Grow two cups of bean sprouts the same way. Put one in the light and one in the dark. Change only one thing!
