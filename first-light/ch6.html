Physicist's notebook

Why protons stop. A proton loses energy as it knocks electrons out of the atoms it passes. The slower it goes, the longer it spends near each atom, so it loses energy faster and faster. Most of its energy is released in the last few millimetres, just before it stops: the Bragg peak. Beyond it, the dose drops almost to zero.

R ≈ 0.0022 × E1.77

R is the range in water (cm) and E is the proton's kinetic energy (MeV). More energy means a longer range.

Spread-out Bragg peak. One peak is only a few millimetres wide. Stacking peaks of lower energies, each with its own weight, gives a flat dose across the whole tumour. The deepest layer gets the biggest weight, because every shallower layer adds its entrance dose on top of the deeper ones.

Protons and X-rays. An X-ray beam fades gradually, so it keeps going out the far side of the head. Protons stop. For a child like Aisyah, whose brain is still developing, less dose to healthy tissue means fewer long-term side effects.

Range uncertainty. The range depends on what the protons pass through. Bone slows them about 1.6 times faster than water; air hardly slows them at all. Planners turn the CT scan into a map of stopping power, but that conversion is never perfect, so ranges can be off by about 3.5% plus 1 mm. Proton plans add a margin at the far edge and avoid stopping a beam right in front of a critical organ.

Game simplifications: dose is calculated along the beam's central axis with a simple sideways spread. Each peak is widened by a ripple filter to about 2.5 mm, and the entrance plateau is approximated. Real plans use several beam angles, pencil-beam or Monte Carlo dose engines, robust optimisation, and a biological factor of 1.1 for protons.
