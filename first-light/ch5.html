    Physicist's notebook

    A radioactive tracer loses half of its activity every half-life, whatever you do with it:

    A = A₀ × (½)^(t ÷ T½)

    For fluorine-18, T½ = 110 minutes. The decay constant is λ = ln 2 ÷ T½ = 0.0063 per minute, so about 0.6% of the atoms decay every minute.

    Why booking order matters: every MBq a patient needs later in the day costs more of the morning's delivery, because some of it decays while it waits. Give the biggest doses first, while the vial is strongest, and keep the bookings close together.

    Why the cyclotron is nearby: F-18 is made in a cyclotron by firing protons at oxygen-18 water. With a 110-minute half-life, it must be made a short drive from the hospital, early in the morning. You cannot make it last week and store it.

    Radiation safety: injected patients are slightly radioactive for a few hours, so they rest in their own uptake room and wait apart from other patients, like the separate entrance on the department floor plan.

    Decay lab

    Each dot is one atom. Every atom decays at a random moment, yet together they follow the half-life curve.

    Game simplifications: doses use 3.7 MBq per kg (between 120 and 370 MBq), quality control takes 20 minutes, there is one scanner, and activity left in syringes and tubing is ignored. Real departments also measure every dose in a calibrated activity meter before injection.
