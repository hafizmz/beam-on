Physicist's notebook

Why the weather changes the reading. An ionisation chamber is a tiny pocket of air between two electrodes. Radiation knocks electrons off air molecules, and the electrometer collects that charge in nanocoulombs (nC). The chamber is open to the room, so the number of air molecules inside follows the gas law, n = PV ÷ RT. Low pressure or warm air means fewer molecules, less charge, and a lower reading for exactly the same dose.

So every reading is corrected back to reference conditions (20 °C, 101.33 kPa):

kTP = (273.15 + T) ÷ (273.15 + 20) × 101.33 ÷ P

Why ±2% matters. A course of treatment is often 15 to 30 daily doses. If the linac gave 3% too much every day, the healthy tissue around the tumour would get 3% too much too. That is why nobody is treated until the morning check is inside tolerance.

Inverse square law, again. Dose falls with the square of the distance from the source. The chamber sits 10 cm deep, so its distance from the source is SSD + 10 cm. At SSD 98.5 cm the chamber is 108.5 cm away instead of 110 cm, giving (110 ÷ 108.5)² = 1.028, about 2.8% extra.

Simplified for the game: real checks follow a dosimetry code such as IAEA TRS-398 and also correct for polarity, ion recombination and the chamber's calibration factor. The small change in depth dose when the SSD moves is ignored, and the tank drawing exaggerates couch movement so you can see it.
